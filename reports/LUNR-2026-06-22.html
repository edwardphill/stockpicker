Rating & Price Target

Rating: Strong Buy | 12-Month Price Target: $40.78 | Current Price: $21.68 | Implied Upside: ~88%

We are initiating coverage of Intuitive Machines (LUNR) with a Strong Buy rating, as we believe the market materially underappreciates the company's contracted revenue trajectory, its entrenched position as a primary commercial vendor to NASA's lunar logistics program, and the structural scarcity value of publicly traded, pure-play lunar/cislunar infrastructure companies — particularly with SpaceX remaining private.

Investment Thesis

The core of our thesis rests on three pillars: (1) LUNR is the only publicly listed company with demonstrated lunar landing capability and a multi-mission NASA CLPS (Commercial Lunar Payload Services) task order pipeline; (2) the company's revenue trajectory has inflected sharply, with TTM revenue of ~$330M reflecting 198.7% year-over-year growth and a remarkable 353.9% quarter-over-quarter acceleration in the growth rate; and (3) the addressable market is expanding beyond NASA toward Department of Defense cislunar domain awareness and commercial data relay services, diversifying the company's customer base in a manner we believe consensus has not yet modeled.

We believe the market is pricing LUNR primarily on near-term profitability metrics — a framework that is inappropriate for a pre-scale infrastructure business with high-visibility contracted backlog. The negative profit margin of -32.7% and negative EBITDA of -$80M reflect program development costs on missions that have not yet reached recurring revenue cadence. As mission frequency increases through 2026–2027, we expect meaningful operating leverage on a largely fixed-cost engineering workforce. Notably, recent volatility in the stock appears driven by broader sector rotation and elevated short interest (26.5% of float) rather than fundamental deterioration — a setup that, in our view, creates asymmetric risk/reward.

The competitive landscape reinforces our conviction. With SpaceX unlikely to IPO near-term and other space equities in early-stage recovery, LUNR offers scarce public-market exposure to the lunar economy. Astrobotic's initial mission difficulties further narrow the field of credible lunar delivery providers. We see LUNR's competitive moat widening with each successful mission.

Valuation

Our $40.78 price target is derived from a blended approach: (1) a 10.5x EV/forward revenue multiple applied to our FY2026E revenue of ~$650M, reflecting expected backlog conversion and new task order wins, and (2) a DCF analysis using a 12% WACC and 3% terminal growth rate on estimated 2030 free cash flow. On a trailing P/S basis, LUNR trades at approximately 10.5x TTM revenue of $330M. While this appears optically rich relative to traditional aerospace & defense peers (typically 2–4x), we believe it is justified — and in fact discounted — relative to high-growth space/satellite comps and the company's revenue growth rate. The forward P/E of -180.7x is not meaningful at this stage; we focus on revenue scale and path to gross margin expansion from the current 9.7% toward our estimated 20%+ by FY2027 as program mix shifts toward higher-margin data services and recurring payload delivery. Debt/equity of 0.62x is manageable and below the median for pre-profitability space peers, suggesting adequate balance sheet runway.

Bear Case Thesis

The market is not "underappreciating" LUNR's contracted pipeline — it is correctly discounting the extreme binary risk of a company whose entire revenue trajectory depends on successful execution of a handful of lunar missions, a domain with a historical failure rate that makes backlog-based valuation perilous. The bull frames a 10.5x forward revenue multiple on $650M FY2026E revenue as conservative, but this revenue estimate embeds heroic assumptions about mission cadence, contract timing, and margin expansion for a company that has completed exactly one lunar landing (with degraded performance — the Odysseus lander tipped on its side). The structural headwind is straightforward: LUNR is a cost-plus/fixed-price government contractor with 9.7% gross margins, a single dominant customer (NASA), and existential operational risk on every mission — yet it trades at a multiple reserved for high-margin, recurring-revenue SaaS businesses. The ~$3.5B market cap implies the market has already priced in significant backlog conversion; the bear case is that this is overpriced, not underpriced.

Challenging the Bull's Assumptions

1. "198.7% YoY revenue growth signals durable trajectory"
Bear alternative: Revenue recognition in mission-based contracts is inherently lumpy and non-recurring. The 353.9% QoQ acceleration likely reflects milestone-based recognition on specific program phases, not organic demand compounding. Base rate for government space contractors shows revenue often plateaus or declines between major program phases. Extrapolating a single year's revenue acceleration into a $650M FY2026 estimate requires assuming near-flawless mission cadence and timely new task order awards — neither of which LUNR controls. NASA CLPS awards are competed; LUNR is not the sole vendor.

2. "Competitive moat widens with each successful mission"
Bear alternative: Astrobotic's Peregrine failure does not permanently eliminate competition — Astrobotic has a second mission (Griffin) funded and in development, and Firefly Aerospace has a CLPS award. The "moat" is narrow and contingent: one LUNR mission failure reverses the perceived advantage entirely. The bull acknowledges this risk but underweights it. The base rate for commercial lunar landers globally is roughly 50% success, and LUNR's sole landing was partially successful at best (loss of solar orientation, abbreviated surface operations).

3. "DoD cislunar domain awareness diversifies the customer base"
Bear alternative: DoD cislunar surveillance is a nascent, unfunded concept with no programmed budget lines at scale. Citing it as a near-term revenue diversifier is speculative. The DoD cislunar market barely exists in procurement terms; LUNR's pipeline remains overwhelmingly NASA-dependent. Customer concentration risk is acute — if NASA's Artemis timeline slips (as it has repeatedly), CLPS mission cadence and associated revenue compress accordingly.

4. "10.5x forward revenue is justified vs. high-growth space comps"
Bear alternative: The relevant comp set is not high-growth SaaS or satellite broadband plays, but government-dependent aerospace contractors with sub-10% gross margins and binary mission risk. Comparable defense/space services businesses (e.g., Rocket Lab at higher margins and diversified revenue) trade at 6–8x forward revenue. Applying a premium to LUNR for "scarcity value" as a lunar pure-play is a narrative premium, not a fundamental one. Scarcity is not an investable moat if the underlying cash flows do not materialize.

5. "Short interest of 26.5% creates asymmetric upside"
Bear alternative: Elevated short interest reflects informed skepticism, not uninformed pessimism. The bull's inclusion of short squeeze mechanics as a formal catalyst is a retail framing that conflates price action with fundamental value creation. High short interest in pre-profit companies with binary risk events is a feature, not a bug — it reflects the probability distribution correctly.

6. "Debt/equity of 0.62x is manageable; no near-term dilution risk"
Bear alternative: At -$80M EBITDA and 9.7% gross margins, the cash burn rate matters more than the leverage ratio. LUNR will likely require additional capital raises before reaching breakeven (estimated late FY2027/early FY2028 by the bull, which itself may be optimistic). Any equity raise at current or lower prices would be meaningfully dilutive to a $3.5B market cap company with limited float. The bull's "no immediate dilution risk" language is imprecise — the question is not whether dilution occurs, but when and at what price.

Downside Scenario & Valuation

We model two bear scenarios:

Scenario A — Mission delay / margin stagnation (60% probability in bear case): FY2026 revenue comes in at $400–450M (vs. bull's $650M) due to mission slippage and slower-than-expected task order conversion. Gross margins remain at ~10%. The market re-rates LUNR toward a government services multiple of 4–5x forward revenue. At 4.5x on $425M revenue, EV = ~$1.9B. Adjusting for net debt and potential dilutive capital raise (~15% share increase), this implies a stock price of approximately $9.50–$11.00, representing 50–55% downside.

Scenario B — Mission failure (25% probability in bear case): Next CLPS mission experiences anomaly. NASA diversifies future awards away from LUNR. Revenue reverts toward $200–250M as backlog conversion stalls. Multiple compresses to 3x on impaired forward revenue. EV = ~$650M. Stock trades to $3.50–$5.00, representing 75–85% downside. In this scenario, a dilutive capital raise becomes near-certain and likely punitive.

Probability-weighted bear case price: ~$9.00, roughly 58% below current levels.

Verdict

Monitor. The debate resolves on two specific data points: (1) the outcome of the next CLPS mission in 2H 2026, which is a genuine binary event that determines whether backlog conversion assumptions hold, and (2) the Q3 FY2026 earnings report (August 6, 2026), where gross margin trajectory above or below 15% will confirm or refute the operating leverage thesis — until both are known, the risk/reward is not asymmetrically favorable but rather symmetrically binary, and position sizing should reflect a wide distribution of outcomes, not a point estimate of 88% upside.
